The first movie ever was streamed over the internet in 1993. It was the cult-followed Wax: Or the Discovery of Television Among the Bees. According to a Times article, it took up until almost halfway through the digital premiere for engineers to actually find and then transmit the internet signal playing the movie onto their computers. The color-image was reduced to black and white, displayed in an 8-inch window, the audio cutting in and out whenever the internet was overloaded with data traffic, and playing at a halting 2 frames per second, instead of the 24 frame rate of a typical broadcast. (Markoff) While it would take the rest of the decade to improve streaming’s quality, the new technology proved extremely valuable, and thus live-streaming became the first mainstream use of streaming technology. The first livestream happened about a month after the fiasco of Wax; a concert of the American rock band Severe Tire Damage. The drummer of the band, Mark Weiser, worked at the Xerox Pablo Alto Research Center, where the first streaming technology was being developed. Testing out new streaming software MBone, the band decided to stream their concert, which would then lead to The Rolling Stones wanting to livestream their concerts the following year. These livestreams were only able to reach a few hundred computers at the time, but still proved to be extremely worthwhile. In 1995, RealNetworks held the first ever public livestream, a baseball game between the New York Yankees and the Seattle Mariners. Then, in 1999, the first presidential webcast was streamed from George Washington University. Even before YouTube came along, MLB.TV started streaming baseball games over the internet as early as 2002. The debut stream was of the August 26, 2002 game between the New York Yankees and the Texas Rangers and amassed 30,000 viewers. Livestreaming would not become part of YouTube’s platform until 2008, but the technology behind it is what ended up being used to engineer the video-sharing platform. Like many others of my generation, I’ve been using YouTube for most of my life. A website I’d go to for music and lyric videos as a kid quickly grew into this mega-social media platform in the early 2010s, with the help of monetization. In 2007, YouTube introduced the YouTube Partner Program (YPP), a new feature where channels meeting a certain criteria could take a percentage of the revenue coming from the ads playing on their videos. Thus began the long-winded spiral into partial damnation. I exaggerate, of course, but a shift definitely started happening once creators could start making money from the content they uploaded, which in turn required more scrutiny on YouTube’s behalf to make sure videos met advertising standards. For a few years, YouTube was a creative, indie online community, where individuals would create and share content with viewers in a more personal and freeform style than anyone was ever used to before. Moderation and scrutinization of content was loose and unrestrictive. It was so new at the time, so unprecedented, and best of all: it was completely free. But regulation of material began happening even before the YPP. YouTube managed to avoid any copyright issues for its first few months, despite a lot of copyrighted material being uploaded to the platform, but that all changed by 2006, after the first television clip went viral on the platform. On December 17, 2005, Saturday Night Live premiered they’re first ever digital short, entitled “Lazy Sunday.” It aired at the very end of the episode, and the only way to watch it after its airing was on YouTube. (If not for YouTube there might have been no way to watch the clip after the fact.) Within just a few days, the bootleg video of the skit amassed over 2 million views, and was responsible for bringing a ton of new users to the platform and boosting YouTube into the public eye. On February 16, 2006, YouTube made a post to their community explaining that NBC has asked them to remove the video from the platform; “We know how popular that video is but YouTube respects the rights of copyright holders.” (The Youtube Team) Funnily enough, today you can still watch the skit on YouTube, uploaded on SNL’s own YouTube channel.
